Rethinking Migration and Development

My work sits at the intersection of Political Economy of Development; Migration, Nationalism, and Security; and East Asian Politics and Society.

Dongducheon, Republic of Korea (October 2024)

Research Areas

Political Economy of Development

Development in the Global South, the developmental state in East Asia, and the relationship between migration, population, and development.

Book Project

My first book project, based on my dissertation, Rethinking Migration and Development: The Politics of Emigration in South Korea, asks: why do states push their citizens out of the country, yet continue to claim them afterward?

While South Korea was mobilizing its population for industrialization and restricting the exit of its population, it also institutionalized state-sponsored emigration programs — transnational adoption of babies, agricultural family resettlement to South America, and contract labor exports to West Germany.

Grounded in extensive fieldwork across South Korea and the United States, I argue that South Korea used emigration programs to send abroad burdensome populations while extracting geopolitical and economic value from their departure.

This work contributes to core debates in migration, nationalism, and the political economy of development. Existing scholarship on migration and development emphasizes migration’s impact on the home country through migrants’ agency, such as remittances, technological transfer, or diaspora investment. I show instead that states can actively produce emigration as a coercive and selective developmental strategy. By formalizing emigrations through bilateral agreements, states can monetize departure itself, through capital collected directly from receiving states. In context of geopolitical competition, rival states competing over the same population use emigration and citizenship policy to profit from, while guarding against, the other’s claim to that population abroad.

Rethinking Migration and Development draws on evidence collected from state and community-based archives and interview-based fieldwork in South Korea and the United States. This research has been funded by fellowships including from the Kyujanggak International Center for Korean Studies at Seoul National University, as well as from the East Asian Studies program, the Chloe Center for the Critical Study of Racism, Immigration, and Colonialism, and the Political Science Department at Johns Hopkins University.

Publications & Working Papers

Working Papers

Exile by Paperwork: Ambiguity as Diaspora Governance in Divided Post-Colonial States [+]
Draft available on request
Abstract How do states govern diasporas simultaneously claimed by a rival sovereign? The global processes of decolonization and the Cold War produced "divided states," such as the Republic of Korea (South Korea) and the Democratic People’s Republic of Korea (North Korea), where two or more states claim legitimate representation of one nation. Scholarship on bureaucratic power has shown how states use documents and administrative categories to render populations legible, and more recent work shows how bureaucratic procedure can instead sustain ambiguity, prolonging rather than resolving a population’s status. This literature, however, has focused on populations already present within the territory. This article extends it to a structural condition it has not yet addressed: divided post-colonial statehood, where a rival sovereign’s competing claim makes both inclusion and exclusion of a diaspora costly. I argue that divided states resolve this dilemma through “exile by paperwork”: the deliberate use of bureaucratic instruments of belonging and mobility to exclude diasporic populations from the territory without formally disavowing their membership in the nation. The strategy operates through three mechanisms: jurisdictional claiming, administrative withholding, and disciplinary suspension. Tracing South Korea’s passport, travel-certificate, entry, and citizenship policies toward overseas Koreans, stateless colonial descendants, return migrants, and North Korean defectors from division to the present, this article shows that bureaucratic power operates not only through legibility but also through the deliberate production of ambiguity.

Book Chapters

Eisenhower Administration’s Foreign Policy Decision-Making Process in Response to Syngman Rhee’s Release of Anti-Communist Prisoners [+]
In Chaesung Chun (Ed.), Generality and Specificity in East Asia Regional Order: Theory and Reality. Seoul: Sapyong, 2018.
Abstract This chapter analyzes why the Eisenhower administration decided to compromise with Syngman Rhee’s brinkmanship strategy of releasing anti-communist prisoners during the Korean War, and agreed to sign the Mutual Defense Treaty Between the United States and Korea. It examines the perceptions of five key decision-makers — President Eisenhower, Secretary of State Dulles, Assistant Secretary of State for Far Eastern Affairs Robertson, US Ambassador to Korea Briggs, and UNC Commander-in-Chief Clark — over the period from the release of anti-communist prisoners on June 19, 1953 to the signing of NSC 154/1 on July 3, 1953, which promised the Mutual Defense Treaty. The chapter concludes that decision-makers’ perceptions of Rhee’s intentions and rationale were integral to the outcome.

Thesis

Formulation and Systemization of Transnational Adoption Policy During Park Chung-Hee’s Regime [+]
MA Thesis, Seoul National University, 2019.
Abstract In the aftermath of the Korean War, South Korea aggressively promoted transnational adoption as an alternative to social welfare for children in need. The Park Chung-hee administration established Korea’s transnational adoption policy with the 1961 “Intercountry Adoption Law with Regard to the Orphans,” and continued to send children abroad despite international scrutiny in the 1970s. This study argues that it was under the Park Chung-hee regime that transnational adoption became systemized in Korea, tracing the relationship between the government’s modernization discourse, the collusion of Korean society, international demand for adoption, and the growth of adoption agencies. The government’s modernization discourse — defining progress solely through economic growth — shaped which populations were treated as contributing to that goal and which were not, discriminating against the latter in ways whose effects continue today.
